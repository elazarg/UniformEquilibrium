# Eight signed rewards on two matched pairs in four-player quitting games

At each live date the four players independently choose Quit or Continue using private randomization. The first nonempty quitting coalition S pays the finite vector r(S), and perpetual play and live dates pay zero. Put s_i=r_i({i}), Z_i(S)=r_i(S)−s_i and G_ij=Z_i({j}). Split the players into two pairs A,B. Write m(i) for i's mate and O(i) for the other pair. Suppose G has strictly negative crossed entries G_ij=−b_ij for j in O(i), and some y≥0 satisfies Gy≫0, where ≫ means strict positivity in every coordinate. The four mate entries a_i=G_i,m(i) are then positive. All twelve off-diagonal entries can vary independently.

For each recipient form the two rewards and their common scale

    T_i=Z_i(O(i)),     R_i=Z_i({i}∪O(i)),     K=max_i(|T_i|,√|R_i|).

On every compact set of such singleton matrices there exist K₀,δ,c,C>0 such that, for K>K₀ and |Z_i(S)|≤δK^(|S|−1) at the other 36 nonsingleton positions, the original game has an exact stationary terminal equilibrium. Its four hazards are at most C/K and at least three are at least c/K. Its terminal payoff is a uniform-equilibrium payoff for arbitrary signed s_i. The eight distinguished rewards may have either sign, independently, with arbitrary relative magnitudes including zero. In particular a counterexample in this domain would require K≤C₁ max(1,max_other |Z_i(S)|^(1/(|S|−1))). With the other entries bounded, the entire exterior of a bounded set in these eight coordinates is excluded.

## Stationary comparisons and the limiting field

For a stationary hazard vector q let H_i be the probability all rivals wait, P_i the unconditional centered payoff contribution to Wait from nonempty rival exits on this date, and Q_i the expected centered payoff from forcing Quit. Put F_i=P_i−(1−H_i)Q_i. Against rivals with some positive hazard a finite deadline gives a geometric interpolation of Q_i and the Never payoff P_i/(1−H_i). The required profiles have 0≤q_i<1, F_i≥0 and q_iF_i=0. Every unilateral private strategy gives a mixture of deadlines and Never on the unique live history. With three positive hazards, rivals absorb geometrically for every unilateral replacement. Thus each row shift adds its full constant under all replacements. The expected waiting time against any replacement is uniformly bounded for this fixed game, so the expected finite-average payoff differs from the terminal payoff by O(1/N) at horizon N. This gives the fixed target.

Along any sequence with K→∞ and all other normalized entries tending to zero, extract G and t_i=T_i/K, k_i=R_i/K² as limits. At least one of |t_i|,√|k_i| equals one. The polynomial fields K F(z/K) converge with first derivatives on every compact set to the field

    L_i(z)=a_i z_m−β_i Y + P[t_i−k_i(z_m+Y)],                         (1)
    Y=Σ_{j∈O(i)}z_j,  P=∏_{j∈O(i)}z_j,  β_iY=Σ_{j∈O(i)} b_ij z_j.

Here β_i need only be defined when Y>0. A passive pair contributes two rival quit factors and one mate wait factor. The joining triple has the same factors in Q_i and K(1−H_i) tends to z_m+Y. On a size-d coalition every other entry contributes at most a bounded multiple of K^(1−d)|Z_i(S)|, including derivatives. We seek complementary solutions of the scaled field through its normal map N(w)=L(w₊)+w₋, with coordinatewise positive and negative parts and w₋≤0. Its zeros have z=w₊≥0, L(z)≥0 and z_i L_i(z)=0.

Let Π swap mates. The matrix M=GΠ has diagonal blocks D_A,D_B with positive diagonal elements a_i and crossed blocks −B_AB,−B_BA with strictly positive entries (B_gh)_ij=b_i,m(j). Indeed Gy≫0 forces y and all a_i strictly positive. After scaling columns by Πy, M is strictly row diagonally dominant. The Neumann series for its diagonal minus its nonnegative off-diagonal part and irreducibility give M⁻¹≫0 and G⁻¹≫0. Eliminating one diagonal block gives the two-by-two Schur complement W=D_A−B_AB D_B⁻¹B_BA. Its off-diagonals are negative, its inverse is strictly positive and its diagonals and determinant are strictly positive by the two-by-two inverse formula. The same applies with the pairs exchanged. Every principal two-by-two minor of M is positive by the same column dominance.

The origin is isolated uniformly on compact families of model coefficients. A sequence of nonzero complementary roots tending to zero would normalize to nonzero z≥0 complementary to Gz≥0. The positive inverse would force z≫0 and hence Gz=0, a contradiction. The full nonlinear equation has no nonzero complementary solution with fewer than three positive hazards. A single owner has a negative crossed observer. Two crossed owners have a strictly negative active row and two mates have a strictly positive active row.

## Three positive hazards

Let i be the only active owner in its pair. Set z_i=X, z_m=0. For each opposite owner j the active equations give z_j=d_j X where d_j=b_{m(j),i}/a_{m(j)}. Put γ_i=Σ d_j, κ_i=∏d_j and D_{ui}=Σ_{j∈O(i)}b_uj d_j for u=i,m. Dividing the two remaining rows by X gives the active equation and omitted inequality

    g_i(X)=−D_ii+κ_i X[t_i−k_i γ_i X]=0,
    a_m−D_mi+κ_i X[t_m−k_m(1+γ_i)X]≥0.                            (2)

The first two terms a_m−D_mi are a strictly positive Schur diagonal. For k_i<0, or k_i=0 and t_i>0, g_i has exactly one positive root with positive slope. If both members of a pair have such a root and both k≤0, choose the solitary owner whose root gives the larger opposite total Y=γ_iX. At each owner's own root its t_i−k_iY is strictly positive. For its mate at the chosen larger Y this quantity is still strictly positive, and subtracting k_m X can only increase it. This proves a strict omitted inequality. With a negative row and a mate with k_m=t_m=0, the omitted Schur diagonal suffices as well. Every such strict triple persists under small C¹ perturbations. Its normal-map index is −1 since the opposite two equations eliminate the opposite variables with determinant −a_j a_{m(j)}, and the derivative of Xg_i(X) at the crossing is positive.

In the cases below needing degree, a group has at most one negative k. With fixed nonzero k_i all solitary roots stay bounded as t varies over compact sets. A zero coefficient with t_i≤0 has no solitary root. When k_i=0,t_i>0 and its mate has positive coefficient, a diverging root violates the negative quadratic term of the omitted inequality. With negative mate the preceding strict-triple argument already applies at the target.

## Nonzero joining coefficients in both groups

Suppose each group has some k≠0. Write X=Σ_A z,Y=Σ_B z,Q=∏_A z,P=∏_B z. At a full root a row i in A satisfies

    (a_i−k_iP) z_m/Y=β_i+(k_i−t_i/Y)P.                             (3)

The corresponding relation for B exchanges X,Y and P,Q. The β_i are bounded above and below by positive constants. If X→∞ and some coefficient in B stays bounded away from zero then Q stays bounded, since
(k_j−t_j/(z_m(j)+X))Q=(a_j z_m(j)−β_j X)/(z_m(j)+X). At fixed k while t goes to zero, an unbounded solution must have X,Y both diverging, with their ratio bounded above and below. Otherwise in the smaller group both ratios of its coordinates to the opposite sum tend to zero and (3) forces both its coefficients strictly negative. Thus both products stay bounded and each pair concentrates on one dominant member. If these members are i in A and j in B, the i and j contacts imply k_i,k_j<0, P→b_ij/|k_i|, Q→b_ji/|k_j|. The other two rows give the necessary reciprocal equality

    U_A U_B=1,   U_A=(b_{m(i),j}+s_A b_ij)/(a_{m(i)}−s_A b_ij),
    U_B=(b_{m(j),i}+s_B b_ji)/(a_{m(j)}−s_B b_ji),                 (4)
    s_A=k_{m(i)}/|k_i|,   s_B=k_{m(j)}/|k_j|.

A nonpositive denominator means the corresponding ratio is assigned +∞. Escape requires both to be finite and X/Y→U_A. This proves boundedness along the deformation taking t to zero unless the reciprocal equality (4) holds. It also shows which chart at infinity needs attention at equality.

At t=0, with all k≤0 and at least one strictly negative, a full solution would give Gz≤0, impossible by positive inverse. There is one strict triple per negative row by (2). The total degree of all nonzero roots is therefore minus their number. With all k≥0 and some positive, homotope k to all ones. There are only full roots, and (3) bounds each group member below by a fixed positive fraction of the opposite sum. A coefficient bounded below by a positive constant bounds its opposite product if that sum diverges, and these share bounds exclude divergence. Increase all mate entries to a common value a greater than 3 and twice the largest of the crossed magnitudes and 1; it can also be chosen above all initial mate entries. This preserves a strictly positive M-matrix witness. Then interpolate all crossed magnitudes to one under strict row diagonal dominance. The same compactness applies. At the symmetric endpoint the two members of each pair have identical hazards by (3), say x and y. For v=x/y,

    y²=(av−2)/(v+2)=(a−2v)/(v²(2v+1)).

The first expression increases and the second decreases on their common positive domain. The unique solution is x=y=√((a−2)/3). Its Jacobian has zero diagonal, mate entries D=a−(a−2)/3 and crossed entries −E=−1−4(a−2)/3. The eigenvalues D−2E=4−2a, D+2E,−D,−D give index −1.

The following deformations at t=0 compute nonzero total degree on both sides of (4).

* If one group is nonnegative and the other has a negative, lower all positive coefficients to zero with negatives fixed. A fixed negative bounds the nonnegative group's product if its sum diverges. Both members of that group are at least a fixed fraction of the opposite sum. An unbounded sum of its members with bounded product would therefore force the opposite sum to zero. The opposite product would tend to zero and (3) would then bound both members by a constant times that vanishing sum. This excludes escape. The strict triple roots stay bounded by (2) for the fixed negatives.
* With both groups containing a negative and U_A U_B<1, lower all positive coefficients to zero with negatives fixed. At t=0 each group still has a nonnegative row, so its sum is at least a fixed fraction of the opposite sum. The fixed negatives bound both products at a putative escape. The concentration argument forces reciprocal finite factors, each at most its initial value since the ratios in (4) increase with s. This is impossible on the entire path. The endpoint is all nonpositive.
* With U_A U_B>1, hold every positive mate fixed and raise the negative in every such pair to zero. Leave a negative-with-zero pair unchanged. Some positive mate exists because at s_A=s_B=0 the product b_{m(i),j} b_{m(j),i}/(a_{m(i)}a_{m(j)}) is strictly below one by the principal minor of M. There is again a nonnegative row in each pair. Each pair has a coefficient bounded away from zero, either its positive mate or its fixed negative. At escape the negative rows cannot have reached zero, by (3) and bounded products. Their factors in (4) only increase, excluding equality. A solitary negative approaching zero has a fixed positive mate and its omitted inequality in (2) fails at large X. At the endpoint there is a nonnegative group, which was handled above.

Boundedness of complementary z also bounds w₋=−L(z) on its quiet coordinates. The normal map thus has nonzero degree in a sufficiently large shell separated from the isolated origin, and this degree persists under small perturbations on its boundary.

## A finite cutoff at reciprocal equality

At equality choose the negative rows i,j so that k_{m(j)}>0, exchanging the groups if needed. Vary that positive coefficient by μ about zero. An escape must concentrate on exactly i,j and meet the limits preceding (4), uniformly as μ→0. All such roots are full. Use coordinates h=1/(X+Y), v=X/Y, P,Q near these limits. The larger A coordinate is Xφ(Q/X²), its mate Q/[Xφ(Q/X²)], with φ(u)=(1+√(1−4u))/2. The same formulas with Y,P recover B. After dividing A rows by Y and B rows by X their smooth extensions at h=0, in order i,j,m(i),m(j), are

    |k_i|P−b_ij,   |k_j|Q−b_ji,
    (a_{m(i)}−k_{m(i)}P)v−b_{m(i),j}−k_{m(i)}P,
    (a_{m(j)}−(k_{m(j)}+μ)Q)/v−b_{m(j),i}−(k_{m(j)}+μ)Q.

The first three equations solve for P,Q,v with invertible transverse derivative. The fourth after this substitution is D(h,μ), with D(0,0)=0 and ∂_μD(0,0)=−(1+1/U_A)b_ji/|k_j|≠0.

Here is the degree argument used at both cutoff charts. Choose fixed small h₀ and μ₀ such that the transverse solution is unique on its chart, ∂_μD has fixed strict sign and every outer model root with 0<h≤h₀, |μ|≤μ₀ lies there. The concentration result gives the last assertion by compactness upon shrinking both bounds. On each nonresonant side the total degree away from zero is nonzero. The exterior h<h₀ contributes degree zero whenever D(h₀,μ) and D(0,μ) have the same strict sign. Indeed this region has only full roots, the three transverse residuals supply local coordinates with fixed orientation, and the remaining one-dimensional degree is zero at equal endpoint signs. For fixed μ≠0 a neighborhood of h=0 is free of roots. No monotonicity in h is necessary.

Use the bounded normal-map shell l<Σ w₊<1/h₀, w_i>−B, with l sufficiently small and B larger than all |L_i|+1 on its compact z region and parameter interval. The inner and negative faces are clear of zeros. On the outer face, only the transverse patch can contain zeros for a sufficiently close field. If D(h₀,0)≠0, choose small μ with the same sign of D(0,μ). The cutoff stays clear on the move to that side, so its nonzero finite-shell degree persists at zero parameter. If D(h₀,0)=0, a zero of the actual perturbed field F̃ on the cutoff already suffices. Otherwise its remaining scalar at the transverse solution has a strict sign. Vary F̃ by adding the difference L_μ−L_0 to it. On this fixed compact cutoff its transverse scalar has the same strict parameter derivative sign as D by C¹ closeness. Move to a fixed small μ of the sign that preserves and increases the absolute cutoff residual. At this endpoint the model scalar at h₀ and at zero have the same strict sign. The perturbation can be taken to zero at the endpoint with all shell faces clear. Its shell degree therefore equals the nonzero total degree there and is also nonzero for the original perturbation. The argument includes the case D(h,0) vanishes identically.

## Exactly one pair has zero joining coefficients

Suppose $k_i=k_m=0$ for the two members of A, with some nonzero coefficient in B. Write $X=\sum_A z$, $Y=\sum_B z$, $P=p_B$, $Q=p_A$, and $\beta_h=\sum_{j\in O(h)}b_{hj}z_j/\sum_{j\in O(h)}z_j$. Every $\beta_h$ lies between the minimum and maximum crossed magnitudes. At a full root a row in A has

\[
 (a_h-k_hP)z_{m(h)}/Y=\beta_h+(k_h-t_h/Y)P.                 \tag{5}
\]

The B equations use $(X,Q)$. In particular either sign of a fixed nonzero $k_b$ in B bounds $Q$ when $X\to\infty$, since
\[
 \left(k_b-\frac{t_b}{X+z_{m(b)}}\right)Q
   =\frac{a_b z_{m(b)}-\beta_b X}{X+z_{m(b)}}.
\]

If both $t$ in A are nonpositive, scale all $t$ to zero. The A equations imply $z_{m(h)}=(\beta_h Y-t_h P)/a_h\ge (\min_j b_{hj})Y/a_h$. Divergence of $X$ implies divergence of $Y$ since $P\le Y^2/4$. Both members of A would then diverge, contradicting the preceding bound on $Q$. All full roots are bounded. No A-solitary triple exists. A B-solitary row with fixed nonzero $k$ has bounded positive roots by its quadratic contact. A zero row with $t\le0$ gives none. When $k_b=0$ and $t_b>0$, its possible divergent triple has negative quadratic leading coefficient in the omitted guard if its mate has positive $k$. If the mate has negative $k$, both members are of the directly solvable type $k<0$ or $k=0,t>0$ with $k\le0$, giving a strict triple at the original target. At $t=0$ the all-nonnegative case has degree minus one. If B has a negative row, lower its positive coefficients to zero with the negatives fixed. The same A bound on both its coordinates and the fixed negative coefficient's bound on $Q$ exclude full escape; triples can arise only from the fixed negatives. At the nonpositive endpoint the full contacts give $Gz\le0$, impossible since $G^{-1}\gg0$. Each negative row yields one strict triple by the positive Schur diagonal; their indices are all minus one. This gives nonzero degree in all cases requiring transfer.

Now let $t_i>0$. On its A-solitary support the nonlinear term in the active contact is $t_i p_B$, and the opposite coordinates are the fixed positive multiples $d_h z_i$, where $d_h=b_{m(h),i}/a_{m(h)}$. Hence a unique simple positive contact exists. The omitted mate's residual is $(a_m-\sum_{h\in B} b_{mh}d_h)z_i+t_m P$. The linear coefficient is the strictly positive diagonal of the mate-swapped Schur complement. A nonnegative $t_m$ thus gives a strict triple. It remains to take $t_i>0>t_m$. The direct three-owner comparison already covers two negatives in B and also $k_b<0$ with $k_l=0,t_l\ge0$ for $l=m(b)$.

With exactly one negative $k_b$ in B put $l=m(b)$ and
\[
 v=(b_{mb}-b_{ib}t_m/t_i)/a_m>0.
\]
When B has no negatives use any $v>0$, for example one. Consider $k_i=-\mu t_i$, $k_m=-\mu t_m/(1+v)$ for $\mu\ge0$. At positive $\mu$ this is the previously analyzed two-nonzero-block problem. All A-solitary triples near $\mu=0$ stay bounded: row i's scalar has fixed strictly positive linear coefficient and nonnegative quadratic coefficient, and m's scalar is strictly negative. B's triples are unchanged.

In a full escape along $\mu\to0$, bounded $Y$ would bound both A coordinates by (5), since $P$ stays bounded and the denominators tend to positive constants. For $Y\to\infty$, (5) in row m gives $z_i/Y\ge(\min_j b_{mj})/a_m$ since $k_m\ge0,t_m<0$. Thus $X\to\infty$ and $Q$ stays bounded. In particular $z_m/Y\to0$ and $z_i/X\to1$. Row i gives $t_i P/Y+\mu t_iP=\beta_i+o(1)$, since $\mu t_iP(1+z_m/Y)$ is bounded and nonnegative. It follows that $P/Y$ is bounded, so B has a unique dominant member along a subsequence. Write b for this member. Its own mate satisfies $z_l/X\to0$. Row b forces $k_b Q\to-b_{bi}$, hence $k_b<0$. This rules out every full escape when B has no negative coefficient. Otherwise b was the unique possible one, with $Q\to b_{bi}/|k_b|$ and $t_iP/Y+\mu t_iP\to b_{ib}$. Set $s=-t_m/[t_i(1+v)]$. In row m the limiting denominator is bounded below by $a_m-s b_{ib}=(a_m+b_{mb})/(1+v)>0$. For every split of the two nonnegative summands with sum $b_{ib}$, its unique limiting contact has $z_i/Y\to v$: this follows by substitution using $a_m v=b_{mb}-(t_m/t_i)b_{ib}$ and $k_m(1+v)=-\mu t_m$. Row l now gives the only possible escape condition
\[
 s_B=k_l/|k_b|,\quad a_l-s_B b_{bi}>0,\qquad
 v\,(b_{li}+s_B b_{bi})/(a_l-s_B b_{bi})=1.                \tag{6}
\]
For each fixed positive $\mu$, A's two-nonzero-block concentration ratio is exactly $v=(b_{mb}+s b_{ib})/(a_m-s b_{ib})$. Thus off (6) all full and triple zeros stay uniformly bounded for small $\mu\ge0$, and the nonzero total degree for $\mu>0$ remains valid at zero. If $k_l=0,t_l<0$, the zero mate creates no triple at or toward $k_l=0$ from above since its scalar is strictly negative. The same compactness and degree transfer apply there.

At equality in (6) the only escaping orientation at $\mu=0$ has dominant owners i and b. Use $h=1/(X+Y),u=X/Y,Q=p_A,w=P/Y$. The small A and B shares are respectively $(1-\sqrt{1-4Q/X^2})/2$ and $(1-\sqrt{1-4w/Y})/2$. In particular all $\beta_h$ are smooth in these coordinates down to $h=0$. Divide A, B equations by $Y,X$ respectively. At $h=0$ the four rows i,m,b,l reduce to
\[
 -b_{ib}+t_iw,\qquad a_mu-b_{mb}+t_m w,\qquad
 -b_{bi}-k_bQ,\qquad (a_l-k_lQ)/u-b_{li}-k_lQ .
\]
The first three solve for $w,u,Q$ with invertible transverse Jacobian. After elimination the fourth row's derivative in $t_m$ is nonzero since $du/dt_m=-b_{ib}/(a_m t_i)$ and $a_l-k_lQ>0$. Small positive and negative changes of $t_m$ preserve the opposite signs in A and give the nonzero total degrees proved off that reciprocal equality; the lock uses the corresponding $v$ for each choice of $t_m$. Thus the single-chart finite-cutoff lemma applies, including the case when the unperturbed scalar vanishes identically. It yields on a fixed finite shell a nonzero root of every sufficiently small $C^1$ perturbation. This completes the one-zero-pair case.

## Both joining-coefficient blocks vanish

In this case max |t_i|=1. Write t^A,t^B for the vector t restricted to each recipient pair. Put U=−G⁻¹t^A, V=−G⁻¹t^B, and w(s)=(1−s)U+sV. For d=p_A(y)+p_B(y)>0 set z=y/d. With the trial variable s fixed, the perturbed equation for y and slack λ is

    Gy+(1−s)t^A+s t^B+d e(y/d)=λ,

where e is the C¹-small error in the scaled field. At s=p_A(y)/d, any nonnegative y complementary to λ≥0 is a genuine solution. On compact intervals of trial solutions with d bounded away from zero the implicit function theorem gives a continuous, C¹-close perturbation of y=w(s)+G⁻¹λ, since the limiting Jacobian is G.

If U has a negative coordinate, put x=ΠU. In the B rows x_B=D_B⁻¹ B_BA x_A. A coordinate in x_A must be negative, say x_m=U_i<0. In the two A rows W x_A=−t_A. If (−t)_m≥0 the negative off-diagonal in row m forces x_i<0 as well, and one right-hand entry must be negative by positivity of W⁻¹. Thus we can choose labels with x_m<0 and (−t)_m<0. At s=0 increase only λ_i from zero until y_i=0. The slope (G⁻¹)_ii is positive. At the crossing x_m=0 in x=Πy, and the unchanged row m gives W_mi x_i=(−t)_m<0, so x_i and both B coordinates are strictly positive. The crossing occurs with strictly positive slack and persists under the perturbation by the intermediate value theorem. It gives s=0=p_A/d. Negative V is handled after exchanging pairs.

For U,V≥0, nonzero U has both B coordinates positive and at least one positive A coordinate by the B equations; the reversed statement holds for nonzero V. If both are nonzero, w(s) is strictly positive for 0<s<1 and has three coordinates bounded away from zero throughout [0,1], meaning its third-largest coordinate has positive minimum. Solve the perturbed equation along this interval with λ=0. A negative coordinate at an endpoint must be the vanishing A coordinate on the left or B coordinate on the right. Use its positive own slack derivative to bring it exactly to zero by a small nonnegative slack and obtain a solution there. Otherwise the endpoint vectors are nonnegative. The continuous function s p_B(y₊)−(1−s)p_A(y₊) has opposite weak signs at the two ends. At any interior zero all four coordinates are positive since three already are. At an endpoint zero y is already nonnegative. This gives the desired exact ratio in either case. For nonzero U and V=0 truncate the interval to [0,s_R] with p_A(U)/(p_A(U)+p_B(U))<s_R<1. The scalar is strictly positive at the right end for the limit and for close perturbations. Exchange the groups if U=0. They cannot both vanish.

## Uniformity and the matrix boundary

If uniform constants failed, one could take games with K>n and other normalized coefficients ≤1/n, having no solution whose maximum scaled hazard is ≤n and whose third-largest is ≥1/n. Compactness provides a limiting singleton matrix and direction. The strict three-player root, nonzero shell degree, cutoff solution or ratio argument provides actual roots in a fixed bounded set away from zero along that subsequence. Any limit has at least three positive entries by (1) and the support calculation. This contradiction proves the claimed uniformity.

There is a converse for the scale guaranteed by the theorem. Fix a pairing and a hollow matrix G such that each principal triple is nonsingular and the only z≥0 complementary to Gz≥0 is zero (the R₀ condition). Suppose that for each player i, along each of the two rays T_i=±L, all other nonsingleton rewards zero, arbitrarily large L admit stationary equilibria with maximum hazard ≤C/L and third-largest ≥c/L for some C,c>0. Constants can depend on the ray. Then G has exactly the strict matched signs and positive direction required above. Thus the eight single-coordinate tests already determine on this matrix domain whether the uniform eight-coordinate conclusion can hold.

Indeed take a limit z of Lq on one of these rays, writing D=O(i). It has at least three positive coordinates and is complementary to Gz±e_i p_D(z). The product p_D cannot vanish by R₀. If i is absent, the other three coordinates are positive and solve the homogeneous nonsingular triple equations. Hence i and D are positive, so the support is either the triple C={i}∪D or all four. Both opposite signs cannot give full supports: adding their equations after division by their respective positive products gives a strictly positive kernel vector of G. Nor can both give the triple, since the nonsingular G_CC would require the same inverse column to be strictly positive and strictly negative. Thus one sign gives full support for each i, placing every coordinate vector in the range of G. G is invertible and each column G⁻¹e_i is strictly one-signed. The other sign gives a positive triple. Write σ_i for the common sign of the full inverse column. Its triple vector is a positive scalar multiple of −σ_i G_CC⁻¹e_i. The omitted residual is nonnegative, and block inversion gives

    −G_mC G_CC⁻¹e_i = det(G) (G⁻¹)_mi / det(G_CC).

The omitted residual has the sign of det(G)/det(G_CC) and is nonzero. All the principal triple determinants therefore have the same sign as det(G). For j∈D the active triple equation reads G_ji z_i+G_j,m(j) z_m(j)=0. Its two coefficients must be nonzero and have opposite signs, since otherwise row j of G_CC would be zero. Varying i shows that the two crossed entries in every row have sign opposite to the mate entry. Write G=diag(ε)G₀ with a strict positive-mate, negative-crossed matrix G₀ and row signs ε=±1. A principal triple of G₀ has positive determinant, both directed three-cycle products being positive. Thus every product of three ε's equals sign det(G). All four signs must be the same, say δ. On C the i-column of G₀,CC⁻¹ is strictly negative: its three cofactors are minus the product of the two reciprocal mate entries on D, and the two products of a mate and a crossed entry, divided by the positive determinant. The opposite-sign full and triple roots now force G₀⁻¹≫0. Thus G₀ has a positive direction and its mate-swapped matrix has positive determinant by diagonal dominance and homotopy to its diagonal. Since Π has positive determinant, det G=δ⁴ det G₀>0. Every ε is therefore positive, as asserted. This converse concerns shrinking stationary hazards.

**Exact robust scope.** Fix the pairing and allow the hollow singleton matrix to vary in its twelve entries. An arbitrary matrix is the center of a neighborhood with the stated eight-reward exterior property if and only if it has the strict matched signs and a strictly positive payoff direction. In fact it suffices in the converse to assume throughout some neighborhood just the following weaker property. On each of the eight rays $T_i=±L$ with all other nonsingleton entries zero, some sequence $L→∞$ has stationary equilibria with at least two active players and maximum hazard tending to zero. Neither a rate nor uniformity on that neighborhood is needed for this implication.

To see this, first take the dense subset of matrices with invertible principal blocks of sizes two, three and four, nonzero off-diagonals, and no zero entry in the full inverse. These requirements exclude only zero sets of nonzero polynomials. For example the diagonal cofactors of a hollow four-by-four matrix contain three-cycle monomials, and each off-diagonal cofactor contains a directed path through the other two indices. Along each ray pass to a fixed active set S, divide the active stationary equations by the maximum hazard h, and pass to a nonzero limit x of q/h. If i is absent from S or some member of D is absent, the active equations give $G_{SS}x_S=0$, impossible. Otherwise S contains i and D. All rows of S except i give $x_S=d G_{SS}^{-1}e_i$ with nonzero d. Every entry of this column is nonzero, by the generic assumption in size four and the two-factor cofactors in size three. Hence every supported ratio is strictly positive. The active i equation divided by h reads $d± Lh(x_a x_b+o(1))+o(1)=0$ for $D=\{a,b\}$. Consequently $Lh$ has a positive finite limit on this subsequence. The preceding opposite-support argument applies even without assuming R₀, since any homogeneous limiting vector on three or four positive coordinates would contradict the corresponding invertible principal block. Every generic matrix in the neighborhood must therefore have the asserted matching signs and positive direction.

At the center all entries have the same strict signs, since any zero would allow generic matrices with the wrong sign inside the neighborhood. In mate-swapped coordinates write $M=D-N$, with positive diagonal D and nonnegative irreducible N. The spectral radius of $D^{-1}N$ is at most one by continuity from the generic matrices. At equality, decreasing the four positive diagonal entries by a common factor just below one would give spectral radius strictly above one on an open subset of the neighborhood. This contradicts the conclusion there for generic matrices. Thus the radius is below one. The Neumann series applied to the vector of ones supplies the required positive direction, and the forward theorem applies on a compact neighborhood of the center.

**A ninth coordinate can preclude every shrinking stationary solution.** For each of the 36 other nonsingleton positions, there is an example at the same matching matrix (mate entries 3, crossed entries −1) where allowing that extra reward to grow defeats this stationary conclusion even with arbitrary relative hazard rates. Fix its recipient i, denote its mate by m, and let the opposite pair be a,b. Set own levels to one. Start all centered nonsingleton rewards at zero. Any added passive position has m in its coalition; any added participant position except a crossed pair has m in its rival subset. At such a position raise the passive reward to L or lower the participant reward to −L. In either case the new term in $F_i$ is nonnegative and vanishes whenever $q_m=0$.

Suppose a sequence of nonblank stationary equilibria has maximum hazard h tending to zero. Let x be a subsequential limit of q/h and write $X=x_i+x_m$. When a player has no active rival, its centered comparison field is zero; for all other players the stationary Wait inequality is necessary since all hazards are below one. For the three unchanged rows the inequalities after division by h give $X>0$ (otherwise the m row is strictly negative) and $3x_a,3x_b≥X$. Thus both opposite owners mix, giving $x_a=x_b=X/3$. Pass to a subsequence with $q_m>0$ throughout or with $q_m=0$. In the first case m's contact gives $x_i=2X/9$, $x_m=7X/9$, and $(Gx)_i=5X/3>0$. Player i is active and its additional nonnegative field cannot restore indifference. In the second case $x_i=X$, $(Gx)_i=−2X/3$, and the additional term vanishes exactly. AllNever is excluded by the positive own levels.

For an added crossed participant position on $\{i,a\}$ set its centered reward to −L and set $T_i=−3L$. The exact combined addition to $F_i$ is

    L q_a (1−q_m) [(1−q_b)(1−H_i)−3q_b].

Here $H_i=(1-q_m)(1-q_a)(1-q_b)$. The three unchanged rows give the same two cases. The bracketed expression divided by h tends to $4X/9>0$ in the first case and to $−X/3<0$ in the second. Its sign matches the strictly signed linear row in each case, and i must mix. This exhausts the 36 positions. Every participant in these examples receives at most its own singleton reward, and all own levels are one. Every nonempty support therefore admits weak peeling. The empty-premium-core theorem gives a uniform-equilibrium payoff on these very examples.
